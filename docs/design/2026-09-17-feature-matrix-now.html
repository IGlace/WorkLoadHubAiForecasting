1 · How a forecast is made

The model predicts one number: the hours a member will log in a future week. Everything else is either an input to that prediction or arithmetic done after it. The language model never touches a number; it reads the finished facts and writes prose.

The four ideas that make it work

  1. The target is logged hours per member-week. The truth the model is trained on is the sum of time_logs.hours for a member in a Monday-to-Sunday week. It is what the accuracy check scores too, so training and scoring never disagree.
  2. Every row is a week in a member's past, with the future hidden. A row for week w only contains what was knowable at the end of w (with the five fields section 5 names, which are read as they stand today). Its targets are the hours logged in w+1, w+2, … A booster per horizon learns "given how the last weeks looked, how many hours came h weeks later".
  3. The backtest is the honesty check. Six times, the run pretends it is a past Monday, fits only on rows old enough not to overlap the test week, predicts, and compares with what really got logged. The errors give the mean absolute error and the 10th and 90th percentile offsets that become the band around each forecast.
  4. A week is then placed on days. The predicted week is spread over the weekdays the member actually logs on, in the proportions of their last 13 weeks, skipping holidays and full leave days. Each day's demand is compared with that day's capacity, 8.8 h minus that day's leave hours. Overload is the excess and is never capped.

Who is forecast, and what a team is

A run is for one team, and a team is a team leader and the people who report to them directly: the structure is users.manager_id, and the team's id is the leader's own user id. WorkloadHub's teams and team_members tables hold project teams, ad-hoc groups around one project, and are not read. The role a person has in the forecast is the effective role, derived from users.job_title rather than read from users.role, because the directory leaves that column at MEMBER for almost everyone: a title containing "skill team leader" or "center manager" makes an actor, one containing "team lead" or "lead engineer" makes a leader, anything else a member; then a leader with no counted, active direct report is a member, because leading nobody is not a team. ADMIN, CENTER_MANAGER and VIEWER are the three roles still read from the column, since no title implies them.

A counted person, one the model forecasts, is an active user whose effective role is MEMBER or TEAM_LEADER: the leader is forecast with their team, because team leaders do technical work. A skill team leader manages team leaders, does none, and is never a forecast subject; they run a forecast by choosing one leader beneath them. The whole directory is classified at the start of every run, because whether a leader keeps that role depends on who reports to them. Every counted user's history goes into the model, whichever team is being run; the team decides only whose forecast is produced.

5 · The rules the forecast applies

No feature column is fabricated out of nothing: every one traces back to real table columns. But a handful of rules on the way are the forecast's own, not the application's. Each is named here by the word the lineage diagram and the table use.

history · The assignment date comes from task_history

WorkloadHub stores who a task is assigned to, not when. The module reads the latest task_history row whose new_value (a full name) resolves to the current assignee and takes its changed_at; when no such row exists it falls back to created_date and reports an unresolved assignment in the data-quality facts.

When the application gains a tasks.assigned_at column, the forecast will read it instead.

fresh · Fresh arrivals: the two-day rule

A task is fresh when it was assigned less than two days after being created; otherwise it came from a backlog. Only fresh tasks count in arrival_hrs_lag1..4 and weeks_since_last_arrival; every task counts in arrivals_13w and the shares. The split tells whether work reaches a member fresh or late from the backlog.

family · Task types are grouped into families

Bug, Incident → defect; Story, New Feature, Task, Improvement, Change Request → delivery; Spike, Test, Risk → support; Epic → container; a Sub-task takes its parent's family. All twelve types of the application are named.

assigner · How a task was assigned

The user_id of the same task_history row that gives the assignment date is who assigned the task. Equal to the new assignee: self-picked. Anyone else: assigned. No row, or no user on it: unknown, and the task is left out of the two shares. This is why every assignment must write a history row with its user_id.

remaining · Remaining hours: two definitions on purpose

The features use estimate − hours logged by the row's week for open_remaining_hrs and due_hrs_h, because a training row must see what was known then and remaining_estimate_hrs has no history. The facts the leader reads (open_est_hours, due_hours, the pressure figures) use the stored remaining_estimate_hrs, the application's own number.

blank · Blank instead of invented

Every ratio whose denominator is zero, and "weeks since" when there was no event, are left empty. XGBoost's trees learn a default direction for a missing value at each split from the training rows that lacked it, so a blank is its own branch, not a zero and not an average. Counts and sums stay zero, because zero is their true value. The full list is in the last column of the table in section 4.

as of today · Five fields are read as they stand today

The history replay rewinds assignments, statuses and logs to the row's week, but reopened_from_done, projects.status, due_date, original_estimate_hrs and planned_week have no history. A row for last March "knows" a due date or a plan that may have been set in June. A task's current value is the best available stand-in.

44 h · Capacity is the forecast's own formula

user_capacity and team_capacity are not read. Capacity is whf.default-weekly-hours (44) × the week's working days / 5, minus the member's approved leave hours; a day is 8.8 h minus its leave. The team's capacity is the sum over its members.

team · A team is a leader and their direct reports; its projects are its members' tasks

The team a row describes is the one the member works in: the team they lead if they lead one, otherwise their manager's, keyed by that leader's user id. A member who neither leads nor reports to anyone has a blank team_id. The team's projects are not read from any table: they are the projects its members hold tasks in, and a project counts from the day one of them was first assigned a task in it, so a row built for an earlier week does not see a project the team only picked up later.

teams, team_members and projects.team_id are never read; job_title_role_mappings, the application's own table for the role rule, holds no rows yet and would be the permanent home of it.

planned · Planned weeks are read

planned_hrs_h sums the estimates of the member's open tasks planned for the target week, and the facts carry planned_hours per window so the narrative can say "12 h planned for this window" and the verifier can check it. A planned week is filled only sometimes, and that is enough: an unplanned task simply counts zero.

Recorded by the application but not used by the model

  • tasks.remaining_estimate_hrs is used by the leader-facing facts but not by any feature column (rule "remaining").
  • absences, user_capacity and team_capacity are never read (rule "44 h").
  • personal_leaves rows with status REJECTED or CANCELLED are not loaded; PENDING ones reach the facts only.
  • teams, team_members and projects.team_id are never read: they describe project teams, not who reports to whom (rule "team").
  • users.role is read only for ADMIN, CENTER_MANAGER and VIEWER; the two leader roles come from users.job_title.
  • task_comments, task_attachments, notifications, project_history, job_titles, job_title_role_mappings and user_roles are never read.

6 · How a leave becomes capacity

A leave is one row of personal_leaves: a period from start_date to end_date, a total absence_hours for the whole leave, and optional begin_time and end_time that say it starts or ends on a partial day. Only APPROVED leaves count. One pure function, LeaveDays.expand, turns each into hours per working day:

  1. The days. Every day from start to end that is a weekday and not a confirmed holiday. A leave over a weekend or a holiday costs nothing on those days.
  2. The full day. 44 h / 5 = 8.8 h. There is no per-member figure any more.
  3. The hours. A null or non-positive total means the whole period: every day gets 8.8 h. Otherwise the total is capped at 8.8 h × the number of days and dealt out 8.8 h per day from the first day forward, the last day taking what is left. When only begin_time is set, the leave starts on a partial day, so it is dealt from the last day backward and the first day takes the remainder. A day dealt zero hours is not an absent day.
  4. Two leaves on one day add up, and the sum is capped at 8.8 h.

Those per-day hours are then used in exactly four places, and none of them is a feature of the member's own history:

  1. absence_hrs_h and available_hrs_h. The target week's leave hours over its working days, and 44 h × working days / 5 minus them.
  2. The day placement (offDays). A day whose leave hours reach 8.8 h is a full leave day: no predicted hours land on it and the week's hours move to the other days. A half day is not off: the hours are still placed, against a halved capacity.
  3. The day capacity shown to the leader (dayCapacity). 8.8 h minus that day's leave hours, never below zero; zero on a weekend or holiday.
  4. The facts. Each member's absence_hours per window, and pending_leaves, which lists the member's PENDING leaves that touch the horizon, with start, end, type and hours, served by the get_member_capacity tool: a risk to the window the narrative may mention, never a reduction of capacity.

Nothing in the model's own history columns (lag*, roll_*, arrivals, open work, team, identity) reads leaves at all, so what the model learns from the past does not depend on the leave table; only the target week's availability, the placement on days and the leader's capacity figures do.
