1What this application does

In one sentence: it looks at how your team has worked in the past, estimates how many hours of work each person is likely to have over the next two weeks, compares that with the hours they are actually available, and tells you who is about to be overloaded.

The three quantities

Demand
Hours of work a person is expected to have to do in a given week. It has two halves: work that is already open and assigned to them, and work that has not arrived yet but is expected to, based on history.
Capacity
Hours that person is actually available in that week, after weekends, public holidays, vacations and any manual override are removed. The starting point is 44 hours per full week — 8.8 hours per working day, Monday to Friday.
Overload
The excess: demand minus capacity, when demand is larger. If demand is smaller, overload is zero. It is never negative.
The rule that governs the whole product

Demand is never trimmed to fit capacity. If the arithmetic says a person is expected to have 61 hours of work in a 35-hour week, the app shows 61 hours and reports 26 hours of overload. It does not quietly cap the figure at 35 and pretend everything is fine. Making the problem visible is the entire point of the tool.

Where the AI fits — and where it does not

The application uses your own GitHub Copilot seat. It is important to be precise about what that means, because it is the single most common misunderstanding:

Produced by ordinary program codeProduced by the AI
Every hour figure on every screenSentences
Demand, capacity, overload, the interval rangeThe explanation of why a pattern looks the way it does
Who is overloaded and who is underusedA written risk description for the team
Which forecasting model was chosen, and its scoreSuggested moves of work between people
The history behind each personSuggested changes to someone's available hours

The two figures in the right-hand column — the size of a suggested move and the size of a suggested capacity change — are the only numbers the AI is allowed to originate, because they are proposals, not measurements. Every other number it writes must already exist in the saved facts, and the program checks that, sentence by sentence, before you are shown the text. Section 6 explains what happens when a number fails that check.

Two things worth knowing before you go further

Version 1 runs on generated data

The people, projects and tasks in the current build are dummy data produced by a generator, not your real backlog. Everything in this document describes real, working machinery — but the specific numbers you see on screen today are not about your real team.

The forecast is always two weeks

Every run covers exactly two weeks. If you run it on a Monday, those are this week and next week. On any other day, the current week is already partly spent, so the run covers the next two full weeks instead. There is no other horizon; the number cannot be changed from the screen.

2Where everything runs

Four pieces, and only one arrow that leaves your machine.

The window
What you click on: the desktop application. It draws screens and tables. It performs no calculations of its own — every figure it shows was computed elsewhere and handed to it.
The forecast service
A small program that starts up invisibly with the app and does all the arithmetic: reads the history, fits the models, computes demand, capacity and overload, and saves the result. This is where every number in the product is born.
The database file
A single file on your disk holding people, teams, projects, tasks, the calendar, capacity overrides, and every run ever computed. Nothing is stored anywhere else.
GitHub Copilot
Reached through your own signed-in Copilot command-line tool — your seat, your login. It is contacted only when a narrative is requested, never during the calculation. No key belonging to any other AI provider is used, or can be added.

What crosses the boundary

When — and only when — you ask for the narrative, the service sends Copilot the facts of that one run: the team's name, the members' names, their weekly hours, the overload figures, and thirteen weeks of history per person. The exact content of what was sent is stored back in the database next to the run, so it can be audited later. Nothing else, from any other team or any other run, is ever included.

If you never ask for a narrative

…then nothing leaves the machine at all. The forecast is complete and fully usable without ever contacting Copilot. The AI is an optional layer of explanation on top of a finished result.

3What happens when you press "Run forecast"

Ten stages, all of them ordinary arithmetic and statistics. No AI is involved in any of them.

The stages in words

  1. Collect. Read every task, person, project, holiday, vacation and capacity override from the database.
  2. Decide the two weeks. Monday-start weeks. If today is a Monday, forecast this week and next; otherwise the next two full weeks.
  3. Build a weekly history. Turn the raw task records into one row per person per week: how much work arrived, how much was open, how long things took.
  4. Rehearse. Rewind the clock to several points in the past and test each candidate model on weeks it had never seen. This is the backtest — section 7.
  5. Pick a champion. Score every candidate on the rehearsal and keep the best, with a safety rule that falls back to the simple baseline if none of them beats it.
  6. Predict. Retrain the champion on all the history and predict how many newly arriving hours each person will pick up in each of the two weeks.
  7. Add the work already open. For every task currently assigned and unfinished, estimate how much of it remains and spread that across the working days ahead.
  8. Work out capacity. Count each person's available hours per week, removing weekends, holidays and leave, and applying any override. Demand is open plus new; overload is the excess.
  9. Attach a range. Give each figure a low and a high value, derived from how wrong the champion was during the rehearsal — section 9.
  10. Save. Write the run, both weeks for every person, and a full fact sheet, in one single all-or-nothing transaction.

What "all-or-nothing" means here

The run row, the per-person figures and the fact sheet are written together or not at all. There is no half-saved run. This has one visible consequence: a failed forecast does not appear in the Runs list as a failed run — it does not appear at all. If you pressed the button and no new row showed up, the calculation did not complete.

Everything above is reproducible

The same database and the same date produce the same numbers, every time. There is no randomness in the pipeline, and nothing in these ten stages depends on a network connection, a subscription, or a model being available.

4Asking Copilot for the narrative

"Narrative" simply means the words — the paragraphs that explain the numbers you already have. Asking for it is a separate, optional, second step.

What actually happens

  1. The service reloads the facts that this run already saved. It does not recalculate anything, and it does not read the database freely — it uses the frozen snapshot.
  2. It opens a session on your own signed-in Copilot command-line tool. If you are not signed in, the request stops here with a clear reason.
  3. It hands over three things: a rule sheet ("you may not invent a number"), the facts, and eight read-only tools the model can call to look things up — plus five short in-house guides on this specific domain, so the model uses the right vocabulary.
  4. The model reads what it needs by calling those tools, then writes one structured document: a run summary, a note per person, the team-level risks, suggested moves, suggested capacity changes, and a note on the models used.
  5. The service checks the answer's shape against a strict template. A missing section, an unexpected field or an over-long block fails. On failure it asks again — at most twice in total, then gives up.
  6. The service then checks every number in the prose against the facts. Result: ok or unverified.
  7. The whole exchange is stored: the exact facts sent, the answer received, the check report, the model name and the timings.

The eight tools

The model cannot browse your database. It can only call eight narrow, read-only lookups — "give me this member's two weeks", "give me the team totals", "give me this person's thirteen weeks of history", and so on. Each returns exactly the stored numbers and nothing else. It cannot write, delete, or reach outside this one run.

If it goes wrong

Nothing that goes wrong here can change a single forecast figure — the numbers were saved before Copilot was contacted. Only a status is recorded:

Recorded reasonWhat it means in practice
failed:not_signed_inYour Copilot login has expired or was never completed. Sign in and ask again.
failed:cli_unavailableThe Copilot command-line tool could not be started.
failed:timeoutNo answer within four minutes. Usually transient — ask again.
failed:model_errorCopilot itself returned an error.
failed:invalid_outputThe answer never matched the required shape, twice in a row.
failed:otherAnything else.
You can ask later, and you can ask again

A run saved without a narrative sits at not requested and can be narrated at any time from the Runs list. A run that already has one can be re-narrated. Re-asking replaces the stored record of the previous answer, so if you need to keep an earlier narrative for the record, copy it out first.

5The same run, as a conversation between the parts

Figures 2 and 3 show what is computed. This one shows who talks to whom, and in what order — the view your engineers will recognise fastest.

Reading the diagram

  • Six participants. You, the window you click in, the bridge that holds the security token, the forecast service, the database file, and Copilot.
  • Two requests only. One to compute the forecast, and optionally one to write the narrative. Everything else is internal.
  • The window waits. Both requests are synchronous — the screen shows an elapsed counter, fed by a small progress check it performs once a second. That counter is a display convenience, not a separate job.
  • The order matters. The forecast is committed to disk before Copilot is ever contacted. This is the structural guarantee behind "the AI cannot change a number".
  • A notification. If anyone in the run comes out over capacity, Windows shows a notification when the calculation finishes.

The security token

The forecast service listens only on your own machine, and refuses any request that does not carry a token generated when the app started. Another program on your PC — or anything on your network — cannot read your workload data by talking to it.

6The two status columns, and why a run says "unverified"

The Runs list has two status columns and they mean completely different things. One is about the maths. The other is about the words.

The first column: did the maths finish?

In practice this column only ever reads done. There is no "running" and no "failed", because the row is written only at the very end, after every stage has succeeded. A run that failed leaves no row behind. So: if it is in the list, its numbers are complete and correct arithmetic.

The second column: did the AI write the words, and did they check out?

ValueMeaning
not requestedYou have not asked for a narrative. The numbers are all there; there is simply no prose. This is the normal state of a fresh run.
okEvery number the AI wrote was found in the run's own facts, in the right place.
unverifiedAt least one number in the prose could not be matched. The text is still saved and still shown in full, with an information banner above it.
failed:…No narrative was produced at all. The reason is recorded — see the table in section 4.

Why a run gets marked "unverified"

After the AI returns its text, the program reads back through every sentence, pulls out every number, and asks: does this figure exist in the facts that this particular sentence is allowed to quote? A note about Amina may quote Amina's hours; it may not quote Karim's. If one number cannot be matched, the run is marked unverified.

There are three realistic causes, and they are very different in seriousness:

1 — The AI genuinely got a number wrong

It wrote 46.5 where the facts say 45.6, or rounded differently, or added two numbers together to produce a total that appears nowhere. This is the case the check exists to catch. Trust the table, not the sentence.

2 — The number is real but attributed to the wrong person or field

A perfectly genuine figure quoted under the wrong member, or described as capacity when it is demand. The check is deliberately strict about this, because a right number in the wrong place is exactly as misleading as a wrong number.

3 — Harmless collateral

Whole numbers of 20 or less are skipped by the check unless written with an hours unit ("she has 5 tasks" is ignored; "5 h" is not), precisely so that counting words do not cause false alarms. But percentages, dates written unusually, or a figure quoted from a different week can still trip it. Not every unverified flag is an error of substance.

What to do about it

Unverified affects only the prose. It never affects a single number in the tables or charts. Treat it as: read this text more carefully, and check any figure it quotes against the table beside it. If it looks wrong, ask for the narrative again — the numbers stay exactly as they are.

7The forecasting logic, and the champion model

This is the answer to "what is the champion mechanic". Short version: the app does not trust any one forecasting method. It makes three of them compete on your team's real history, and uses the winner.

The three candidates

Seasonal naive — the baseline
"Next week will look like the same week last year." It looks back 364 days — exactly 52 weeks, so the same day of the week — and repeats what it finds. It is deliberately unsophisticated. Its job is to be the bar the others must clear.
TSB — for stop-start work
Named after the three statisticians who devised it. Built for work that arrives intermittently: nothing for three weeks, then a burst. It tracks two things separately — how often work arrives, and how big it is when it does — which stops it from being fooled by long quiet stretches.
Gradient boosting — the pattern learner
A machine-learning method that builds many small decision rules, each correcting the mistakes of the last. It is given 26 pieces of context per person per week — recent weeks, rolling averages, position in the month, and so on — and learns which combinations tend to precede a busy week.

The rehearsal, and how the winner is chosen

The app rewinds the clock to a point in the past, hides everything after it, and asks each model to forecast the weeks that followed — which it can then mark, because it knows what actually happened. It repeats this at up to six rewind points, two weeks apart. "Up to" matters: a team with a short history gets fewer rewind points, and with less than about three months of usable history it gets none at all.

The score

Each model gets one number. It is its average error divided by the baseline's average error, so:

ScoreReading
1.00Exactly as good as the naive baseline.
0.8020 % less error than the baseline. Good.
1.15Worse than simply repeating last year. Rejected.

Lower is better, and 1.00 is the line that matters. The winner — the champion — is the model with the lowest score, and it is what the Runs screen names.

The safety rule

If the best score is 1.00 or worse, no candidate has earned its place, and the app forces the choice back to the simple baseline. It would rather be predictably plain than confidently wrong.

Three things the champion figure does not tell you
  • It only covers the arriving half of the work. The champion predicts newly arriving hours. The hours already open on assigned tasks are computed by a different method entirely (section 8) and are not scored at all. So "20 % better than the baseline" does not mean the demand figure on screen is 20 % better — often the open half is the larger of the two.
  • The number shown is not always the winner's score. When the safety rule fires, what is reported is the baseline's own score, not the score of the model that was rejected.
  • It is one number for the whole run, not per person. Individuals can be forecast much better or much worse than the headline suggests.

One more thing about how the models are trained

Both the arrival model and the effort model are fitted on everyone in the database who counts toward workload, not just the team you are forecasting — then used to predict only that team. This is deliberate: it gives the models far more history to learn from, which matters most for small teams. The trade-off is that a team whose work is genuinely unlike the rest of the organisation is being forecast partly from other people's patterns.

8How one person's hours are built

Every demand figure in the product is the sum of exactly two things, compared against a third. This is that arithmetic, end to end.

① Work already open

For every unfinished task assigned to the person, the app has to answer two questions: how much work is left in it? and when will that work land?

  • How much is left. It compares the task's own estimate against how long comparable tasks have actually taken in the past, and blends the two. A team that habitually under-estimates has its estimates scaled up; one that over-estimates has them scaled down. The adjustment is capped — never below half, never above two and a half times — so one freak task cannot distort everything. And it never assumes a task is more than 85 % done: even a task that looks finished carries a small remainder.
  • When it lands. A model trained on how long past tasks took predicts a finish date, and the remaining hours are spread evenly across the working days between now and then. Only the hours that land inside the two forecast weeks appear in the result.

New teams are not left without an answer: where there is not enough history, sensible defaults apply — a five-working-day cycle, and estimates trusted as written.

② Work not yet arrived

This is the champion model's output from section 7: the hours of new work this person is expected to pick up in each of the two weeks, based on the pattern of how work has reached them historically.

③ Capacity

Capacity starts from a weekly figure — 44 hours by default — and is scaled by how many of that week's five working days the person is actually available:

capacity for the week = weekly hours × (available working days ÷ 5)

Example: 44 h/week, one public holiday and two days of vacation
         → 2 available days → 44 × 2 ÷ 5 = 17.60 h

Weekends never count. Public holidays and approved leave are removed. Where several rules could apply, the most specific wins: an override for that exact week beats a standing override for that person, which beats the default.

About the 44 hours

44 h is the value the dummy data is seeded with, not a constant enforced at run time. Each person carries their own weekly hours, and part-time members are handled correctly. If a capacity figure looks wrong, the fix is in that person's record or in an override — not in a global setting.

Putting it together

demand   = open hours + newly arriving hours       (never capped)
overload = demand − capacity, or 0 if demand is smaller

A person is listed as overloaded when their overload across the two weeks is above zero — any excess at all, no tolerance band. A person is listed as underused when they have capacity but their two-week demand is below 70 % of it.

Two small honesties about the arithmetic

Rounding. Each component is rounded to two decimals before being added, so a total can differ from the sum you would get by hand by about a hundredth of an hour. Irrelevant for decisions, occasionally visible in a table.

Work beyond the horizon. Open-task hours that would land after the second forecast week are simply not shown — they are outside the window. A person's total open workload can therefore be larger than the two-week demand figure suggests.

9The interval bar: the blue slide and the black pipe

The blue bar is the range the forecast could plausibly fall in. The black pipe is the single figure the app is actually reporting. They are not always centred on each other, and that asymmetry is meaningful.

How to read one row

The black pipe
The forecast demand for that person and week — the same number printed beside it. This is the figure to plan with.
The left end of the blue bar
A plausible low outcome: less new work arrives than expected.
The right end
A plausible high outcome: more arrives than expected.
The width
How predictable this person is. A wide bar means their workload has historically been erratic. A narrow bar means it has been steady. Width is the most useful thing on the chart, and it is why the shared scale matters.

Why the pipe is rarely in the middle

Because only half of the demand carries any uncertainty. Work already open and assigned is known — it is counted as fact. Only the newly-arriving half is a prediction, and only that half gets a range. So a person with a large open backlog and little incoming work has a narrow bar sitting near their demand figure, while a person whose week is mostly speculative gets a wide one. The ends are also allowed to be lopsided: history is often more capable of surprising you upwards than downwards.

Where the range comes from

From the rehearsal in section 7. The app kept every mistake the champion made during the backtest, then took the band that covers the middle 80 % of them — the 10th to the 90th percentile. That band is scaled to the individual by how far their own estimates have historically run from reality, and applied to the arriving half of their demand.

What "80 %" here does and does not mean

It means: 80 % of the errors this model made in rehearsal were inside this range. It is not a calibrated, statistically guaranteed 80 % confidence interval, it has never been tested against how often reality actually lands inside it, and it says nothing about the open-work half. Read it as a well-founded indication of steadiness, not as a probability you could bet on.

If a bar has no blue slide at all

A collapsed bar — a single value, no range — means the rehearsal produced no usable errors to work from, usually because the history was too short to score. It is not a claim of perfect certainty; it is the absence of evidence. Nothing on the screen distinguishes the two cases, so treat a collapsed bar as unknown, not as exact.

10The AI Summary

The AI Summary turns a screen of tables into a short written briefing. It adds no information — it re-tells, in sentences, what the numbers already say, and points out what is worth your attention.

What each part contains

Run summary
A short paragraph on the whole team: the shape of the two weeks, how many people are over capacity, whether the pressure is concentrated or spread. Read this first.
Per-member notes
For each person: a risk level, a one-line summary, any warnings, and observed patterns — "consistently above capacity in the second week", "workload has climbed for four weeks". These appear on the individual member screens.
Team risks
Issues that belong to the team rather than a person: a single point of failure, a project concentrated on one individual, a week where several people peak together. Each has a title, a detail, and the members it concerns.
Suggested moves
Proposals to shift hours from one person to another in a specific week, with a reason. Covered fully in section 11.
Suggested capacity changes
Proposals to change someone's available hours — overtime, or reduced load — rather than move the work.
Model notes
A plain-language remark about which model won and how confident the forecast is. This is the AI describing the champion mechanic from section 7 in its own words.

How much to trust it

The figures inside it are checked

Every number in the prose has been matched against the run's own facts before you see it. When the panel reads ok, every figure it quotes is one the program itself calculated. That is a much stronger guarantee than a normal chatbot answer.

The reasoning inside it is not checked

Only figures are verified. Causal claims — "because the Atlas migration landed" — are the model's interpretation, produced from the numbers alone. It does not know about your reorganisation, the customer who went quiet, or the person about to go on parental leave. Read the interpretations as a well-informed colleague's first reading, not as findings.

If the panel carries an unverified banner, section 6 explains what that means and what to do about it. The text is still shown in full — nothing is hidden from you.

11Rebalancing

The Rebalancing page suggests moving hours from people who are over capacity to people who have room. It is a discussion aid. It changes nothing.

The three steps

  1. The program sorts everyone into two lists. Overloaded: two-week overload above zero. Underused: has capacity, and two-week demand below 70 % of it. These lists are pure arithmetic — no judgement involved.
  2. Copilot is handed those lists, together with each person's weekly figures and their thirteen weeks of history.
  3. Copilot writes the moves — from whom, to whom, which week, how many hours, and why. Each proposal is then validated against the facts: the source must really be over capacity in that week, the destination must really have room, and the hours must not exceed the room available.

What is checked, and what is not

✓ Checked by the program⚠ Not checked — your judgement
The source person is genuinely overloaded that weekWhether the receiving person can actually do that work
The receiving person genuinely has room that weekWhether the task can be handed over at all mid-flight
The hours moved do not exceed the room availableClient, security or contractual constraints
Both people are in this runThe cost of the handover itself
Every figure quoted exists in the factsLeave, notice periods, anything not in the database

Two limits to be aware of

There is no optimiser behind this

The app does not solve an assignment problem or search for a best allocation. The two lists are arithmetic; the moves themselves are written by the language model and checked afterwards. Expect sensible, plausible suggestions — not a mathematically optimal plan, and not a complete one.

Suggestions are validated one at a time

Each move is checked on its own, against the original figures. Three separate moves that each individually fit into the same person's spare capacity will each pass the check — and together will overload them. Look at the list as a whole before acting on any of it.

Nothing is applied

There is no button that moves work. No task is reassigned, no schedule changes, no one is notified. The page produces a list you can take into a conversation. Every re-run recomputes it from scratch.

12What to trust, and what to treat with care

This section exists so that nobody has to discover these things the hard way. None of it is a defect; all of it is a consequence of a deliberate design choice in version 1.

Solid

  • The arithmetic. Demand, capacity and overload are ordinary, reproducible calculations, covered by tests including property tests on the invariants. The same data and date always give the same answer.
  • The separation. The AI cannot alter a forecast figure. The numbers are on disk before it is contacted, and it has only read-only access afterwards.
  • The auditability. Every narrative stores the exact facts that were sent, the answer received, and the verification report.
  • The privacy boundary. Nothing leaves the machine except one narrative request, on your own Copilot seat, and only when you ask.

Treat with care

The reported score may be the baseline's, not the winner'sWhen the safety rule forces the fallback, the figure shown belongs to the baseline model.
Short history means a weak forecastFewer than about three months of usable weeks means no rehearsal at all — no scoring, and no interval range.
The interval is indicative, not calibratedNobody has measured how often reality lands inside it. Use the width to compare people, not as a probability.
A collapsed interval means "unknown", not "certain"Nothing on screen distinguishes an absent range from a genuinely tiny one.
Work beyond week two is not shownOpen hours landing after the horizon are dropped from the figures.

Re-narrating overwrites the previous audit recordCopy out a narrative you need to keep before asking again.

"Today" is taken in UTCLate in the evening in a positive-offset timezone, a run may date itself to tomorrow. Set the date explicitly if it matters.

What version 1 does not attempt

  • It does not schedule anything, assign anything or notify anyone.
  • It does not model skills or seniority — an hour is an hour, whoever does it.
  • It does not know about work that lives outside the database.
  • It does not learn from your feedback; each run starts from the data alone.

13Glossary

The second status column in the Runs list: not requested, ok, unverified, or failed with a reason. About the words only, never the numbers.
Arrival model
The model that predicts how many hours of new work reach a person in a week. One of three candidates; the winner is the champion.
Backtest / rehearsal
Rewinding to a point in the past, hiding everything after it, and marking each model on what it predicted for the weeks that followed.
Capacity
Hours a person is available in a week, after weekends, holidays, leave and overrides.
Champion
The forecasting model that won the rehearsal and was used for this run. Named on the Runs screen with its score.

Hours of work a person is expected to have: open work plus newly arriving work. Never capped by capacity.
Department / team / member
The three levels: a department is led by a skill team leader and contains teams; a team is led by a team leader and contains members.
Effort model
The separate model that estimates how much work remains in an already-open task, and when it will land.
Facts
The frozen snapshot of everything a run computed, saved alongside it. The only thing Copilot is allowed to read, and the reference every quoted number is checked against.
Horizon
The two weeks a run covers. Always two, always starting on a Monday.
Interval
The blue bar: a plausible low and high value around the forecast, derived from rehearsal errors. See section 9.
Narrative
The written text produced by Copilot for a run — the AI Summary, the per-member notes and the rebalancing suggestions, together.
Overload
Demand minus capacity, when demand is larger; otherwise zero.
Overridden capacity
A manually set number of weekly hours for a person, either for one specific week or standing until changed.
Run
One complete forecast for one team on one date, saved permanently with all its figures and facts.
Score (MASE)
A model's average error divided by the naive baseline's average error. Lower is better; 1.00 means no better than the baseline.
Seasonal naive
The baseline model: repeat what happened 52 weeks ago. The bar the other models must clear.
Underused
Has capacity, and two-week demand below 70 % of it. The candidate list for receiving work in Rebalancing.
Unverified
At least one number in the narrative could not be matched to the facts. The text is still shown; the figures in the tables are unaffected. See section 6.

With nothing installed

PlantUML online

Paste a .puml file into the PlantUML web server. Note that this uploads the diagram text to a public service — use a local render instead if that is not acceptable.

Locally, in PowerShell

# PlantUML (needs Java)

mmdc -i 04-sequence.mmd -o 04-sequence.svg
